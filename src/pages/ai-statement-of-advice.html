Can AI write a Statement of Advice?

Technology can prepare much of the groundwork for a Statement of Advice (SOA). It does not change who is responsible for it.

By Theianex · Published 16 November 2026 · General information, not legal advice

The short answer

  • AI can help prepare and check parts of an SOA. Reading documents, organising client information, flagging gaps and drafting from verified data are all realistic uses.
  • It cannot take over the adviser’s obligations. The recommendations, the best interests duty and the sign-off stay with the adviser and the licensee.
  • ASIC’s message to licensees is that existing obligations apply to AI use, and that governance has to keep pace with adoption.
  • Every AI-assisted document needs a named person who checks it against the client file and the adviser’s instructions.

What AI can do in SOA preparation

An SOA starts as a pile of client documents: fact-finds, statements, insurance schedules, meeting notes and the adviser’s instructions. Much of the early work is reading, extracting and consolidating. That is where AI helps most.

  • Read and extract. Pull names, dates, entities, income, assets, liabilities and insurance and super details from supported documents.
  • Organise. Put that information into one structured client file, with each value traceable to its source document.
  • Flag gaps. Surface missing information and figures that differ between documents, so they become adviser queries early rather than delays late.
  • Draft and check. Carry verified information into SOA sections and run preliminary consistency checks.

What stays with people

The Corporations Act 2001 sets out when an SOA must be given to a retail client and what it must contain. It also requires the adviser to act in the client’s best interests (s 961B) and to provide appropriate advice (s 961G). A tool does not hold those duties. The adviser and the licensee do.

  • The recommendations. Deciding what to recommend, and why, is the adviser’s judgement.
  • Accuracy. AI can produce fluent text that the client file does not support. Every figure and statement needs checking against the source documents.
  • Approval. Someone accountable reads the finished document and signs it off.

What ASIC has said

In REP 798, Beware the gap, released on 29 October 2024, ASIC reported on how 23 AFS and credit licensees were using and planning to use AI, across banking, credit, insurance and financial advice. Its central finding was that AI adoption was running ahead of governance, and that not every licensee had adequate arrangements to manage the risks. The report also pointed to reliance on third-party AI models and reminded licensees that existing obligations still apply, including accountability for functions they outsource.

The practical reading for an advice practice: using AI, or using a provider that does, is permitted, but it has to sit inside your licensee’s risk and compliance arrangements. compliance lawyer to confirm this summary of REP 798 and the statutory references before launch

Six checks for any AI-assisted SOA process

  1. Who reviews every document? A named human, before it reaches the adviser.
  2. Can each figure be traced? Values should point back to a source document.
  3. Whose instructions drive the work? The adviser’s scope and instructions, not the AI’s own judgement.
  4. Where does client information go? Which providers receive it, where, and whether they retain it. If it leaves Australia, the Privacy Act’s cross-border disclosure rules (APP 8) may apply.
  5. Is it inside your licensee’s policies? Check approvals, record-keeping and any disclosure expectations.
  6. Do open queries reach the adviser? Unresolved gaps should be listed, not filled by assumption.
